The Clockmaker's Riddle

A midnight mystery in an old workshop ends with a surprising reveal that turns the seeker into the sought.

Story

I was the only one left in the old workshop when the bell chimed midnight. The old oak floor creaked under my feet, and the faint smell of oil and brass wrapped around me like a familiar blanket. I had come to investigate a curious theft: the workshop’s prized antique pocket watch, said to have belonged to the town’s founder, had vanished from its locked display case.

I had been called by the town clerk, a nervous woman named Mrs. Alder, who claimed the watch was a symbol of heritage and that its disappearance was a crime of the highest order. I nodded, tucked my recorder into my pocket, and stepped inside the dim room. The case was a simple wooden box, its lid locked with a brass clasp. I examined the lock, the hinges, the tiny hairs of dust on the surface. The lock had not been tampered with. Yet the watch was gone.

I turned my attention to the room itself. There were no footprints, no signs of forced entry. The only evidence was a single, faint smear of black paint on the wall, a streak that ran from the ceiling to the floor near the back door. I followed the line. It led to a narrow alcove where a stack of old journals and tools lay scattered. The paint had been fresh, the color matching the paint on the workshop walls. I crouched to examine it closely.

The paint was a dark charcoal, a shade I recognized from the old wall paint in the attic of the town hall. It was the same paint that Mrs. Alder used to paint her office walls. I felt a chill run down my spine. Had she hidden the watch? I dug through the stack of journals, searching for any mention of the watch. My fingers brushed over a page dated back to 1863, a letter written by the town founder himself. It described the watch’s origin and how it was to be placed in the town’s hall after his death. It also mentioned a secret compartment in the founder’s study where the watch was kept.

I followed the letter’s instructions and went to the town hall. The founder’s study was locked behind a heavy oak door. I forced it open, and the room was filled with dust and old books. In the corner, a wooden box sat on a shelf, its lid slightly ajar. Inside, wrapped in a velvet cloth, was the missing watch. The watch was pristine, its face gleaming, its hands still ticking.

I turned to leave, but my recorder suddenly crackled. I heard a whisper, faint but unmistakable, in my own voice: "You shouldn’t have come here." I froze. The whisper grew louder, and I realized it was coming from the watch itself. It began to speak, its voice a gentle tick-tock, like the steady rhythm of a heart. It told me that it had been hidden by the founder to protect it from those who would misuse its time-traveling power. It revealed that the watch had been the key to the town’s future, and it could only be trusted to those who understood its purpose.

I stared at the watch in disbelief. It was a strange, almost impossible situation: a talking watch. But then I remembered a strange phrase Mrs. Alder had repeated in her office: "Time is a thief, and the thief is me." I realized the watch had been hidden to protect her, to keep her from being consumed by the relentless passage of time. The thief, the one who stole the watch, was the one who had hidden it: Mrs. Alder herself, to safeguard her own future.

I left the workshop with the watch in my pocket, knowing that the truth would stay hidden. I would tell the town the watch had vanished, but I would also know the deeper truth: the thief had been the one who kept the watch, and the watch had been the one who kept the thief. The mystery was solved, but the twist was that the thief and the treasure were one and the same, and the town’s heritage was protected by the very thing it feared.
